## The Clamping Kit

Where a vise is a self-contained fixture, a clamping kit builds a clamp directly on the machine table using the table's T-slots. A basic kit has four parts working together:

- **T-bolts or studs** — thread into a nut captured in the table's T-slot, providing the anchor point the clamp pulls against.
- **Step blocks** — a stack of stepped supports that set the height on the far side of the clamp, opposite the part.
- **Strap clamps (finger clamps)** — a bar with a slot, spanning from the stud through the strap and down onto the part, with the far end resting on a step block.
- **Nuts and washers** — tension the strap against the part when the stud nut is tightened.

## Leverage: Where You Put the Stud Matters

A strap clamp is a lever. The stud is the pivot the strap pulls down against as the nut is tightened, the part is one contact point, and the step block is the other. Moving the stud closer to the part and farther from the step block increases the downward force delivered to the part for the same nut torque; moving it toward the step block reduces force at the part.

:::key-concept
Position the stud as close to the part as the clamp and part geometry allow. A stud placed near the step block instead of the part wastes most of its clamping force pressing on the step block rather than the workpiece.
:::

## Setting Step Block Height

The step block's job is to keep the strap level so its clamping force points straight down into the part and its locators, not at an angle that could push the part sideways.

- Choose (or adjust, on an adjustable step block) a step height that puts the strap roughly parallel to the table with the part in place.
- Use a matched pair of step blocks at the same height when clamping is symmetric — mismatched heights tilt the strap and send part of the clamping force sideways instead of down.
- Avoid the strap overhanging past the edge of the step block; an unsupported strap end can rock or slip off under load.

:::warning
A strap clamp angled downward toward the step block (because the step is set too low) directs part of its clamping force sideways, toward the part's edge, rather than straight down into the locators. On a light or poorly-supported part, that sideways component can push it off its locating surfaces during the cut.
:::

## Common Strap-Clamp Mistakes

- **Stud too far from the part** — most clamping force is lost to the step block instead of reaching the workpiece.
- **Clamping over an unsupported span** — the same distortion risk covered in the clamping-force lesson; back thin sections with a support, not just a strap.
- **T-nut barely engaged in the slot** — a stud threaded into a T-nut with only a couple of threads engaged can pull out or strip under load; seat the nut with adequate thread engagement before tightening.
- **Tightening one clamp fully before the others** — spreads uneven load across the part; snug all clamps first, then bring each to final torque in sequence.

## Quick-Action Clamps

For lower-force, high-changeover work, cam-action or toggle clamps replace the stud-and-nut strap with a lever that locks in place in one motion. They apply less force than a properly torqued strap clamp but save significant time on jobs where parts are loaded and unloaded frequently and the cutting loads are modest.

:::pro-tip
Match the clamp style to the job, not just what's already on the table. A strap-and-step-block setup gives the most force and the most flexibility in placement; a cam clamp gives speed. Mixing both — cam clamps for quick initial hold, then a strap for final security on the heaviest cut — is common on higher-force setups with frequent part changes.
:::

## Self-Check Questions

**1. Why does moving the stud closer to the part increase the clamping force delivered to it?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The strap acts as a lever pivoting at the stud. Moving the stud closer to the part and farther from the step block shifts more of the clamping force to the part side of the lever for the same nut torque.
</details>

**2. What problem does a step block set too low (angling the strap downward toward it) cause?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

It directs part of the clamping force sideways toward the part's edge instead of straight down, which can push a lightly-supported part off its locators during the cut.
</details>

**3. Why should a strap clamp not overhang past the edge of its step block?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

An unsupported strap end can rock or slip off the step block under clamping load, losing consistent, level force on the part.
</details>

**4. Why should you snug all clamps in a setup before fully torquing any single one?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Fully tightening one clamp first concentrates deflection there and can shift the part before the remaining clamps engage, spreading load unevenly. Snugging all first, then torquing in sequence, keeps the load even.
</details>

**5. When is a cam-action or toggle clamp preferred over a strap clamp?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

On jobs with frequent part changes and modest cutting loads, where the speed of a one-motion lock matters more than the higher clamping force a properly torqued strap clamp can deliver.
</details>
