## The Milling Vise as a Locating Fixture

A milling vise isn't just a clamp — it's a 3-2-1 locating scheme built into one tool. The vise floor and the fixed jaw provide two of the three planes; the movable jaw only ever clamps, it never locates, because its position depends on how the part happens to sit when the screw is tightened.

## Vise Types

**Kurt-style vises** are the shop-floor default: a fixed jaw, a movable jaw driven by a screw, and hardened, ground vise ways bolted to a subplate or directly to the table. Robust, high clamping force, and a known, repeatable geometry once indicated in.

**Self-centering vises** move both jaws symmetrically toward the centerline at once, so the part's centerline stays fixed regardless of stock width. This matters when a feature must be machined symmetric about the part's center without re-indicating for every different stock size — die sets, symmetric brackets, and repeat jobs with varying raw stock width all benefit.

**5-axis vises** are low-profile, often with dovetail or modular baseplates, designed so a rotary/tilt table can present the part to the spindle from many angles without the vise body itself colliding with the spindle, tool, or table. Standard Kurt-style vise height and width are usually too tall and too wide for that clearance.

:::pro-tip
Match the vise to the machine, not just the part. A tall vise that clears easily on a 3-axis mill can collide with the head or a rotary table the moment you tilt into a 5-axis setup — check swept clearance before you commit to a fixture.
:::

## Indicating the Vise

Before any part goes in, the vise itself must be trued to the machine:

1. Clean the table and vise base of chips and burrs — a single chip under the vise translates directly into a tilted locating plane.
2. Mount the vise and snug the hold-down bolts.
3. Sweep a dial indicator along the solid (fixed) jaw's face, moving the machine along the axis the jaw should run parallel to.
4. Tap the vise square using a soft mallet, re-check, and only then fully torque the hold-down bolts.
5. Re-check after final torque — tightening the bolts can shift the vise slightly.

:::warning
A vise that reads square before final torque but shifts as the bolts are tightened will locate every part in that setup slightly off-angle. Always take the final indicator reading after the vise is fully torqued down, not before.
:::

## Parallels and Seating the Part

Parallels raise the part off the vise floor so the jaws clear the vise body and cutting tools clear the vise floor. Getting them wrong is one of the most common sources of a part that machines flat on top but comes out with a bow or a step:

- Select parallels tall enough for tool clearance but not so tall that the part sits unstably high.
- Before final clamping, tap the part down onto the parallels with a soft mallet while the vise is only lightly clamped — this seats the part fully against the parallels and closes any gap.
- Confirm the part is actually seated (no rock, no visible gap) before applying full clamping force. Clamping hard on a part that's bridging a gap over the parallels bows it exactly the way an unsupported span does — see the previous lesson on clamping-induced distortion.
- For repeat jobs, a fixed vise stop against one end of the stock gives repeatable length positioning without re-measuring every part.

## Clamping Force at the Vise

Vise clamping force comes from screw torque, and more torque is not automatically better:

- Enough force to hold the part against the cutting loads it will see, with margin for interrupted cuts.
- Too much force on a thin or narrow part can bow it between the jaws before the first cut is even made — the same sprung-part risk covered in the previous lesson, just generated by the vise screw instead of a strap clamp.
- Recheck vise way and screw condition periodically; worn, dry, or damaged ways change how much actual clamping force a given amount of handle torque produces.

## Self-Check Questions

**1. Which two vise elements act as locating planes, and which element only clamps?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The vise floor and the fixed (solid) jaw locate the part. The movable jaw only clamps — its final position depends on the part, so it cannot be trusted to locate.
</details>

**2. Why must you take the final indicator reading on a vise after the hold-down bolts are fully torqued, not before?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Tightening the hold-down bolts can shift the vise slightly, so a reading taken before final torque may no longer be accurate once the vise is fully secured.
</details>

**3. What problem does tapping a part down onto parallels before clamping prevent?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

It closes any gap between the part and the parallels, preventing the part from bridging a gap and bowing when full clamping force is applied.
</details>

**4. What advantage does a self-centering vise offer that a Kurt-style vise doesn't?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Both jaws move symmetrically toward the centerline, so the part's centerline stays fixed regardless of stock width — useful for symmetric features without re-indicating for every stock size.
</details>

**5. Why can over-tightening a vise distort a part before any cutting happens?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Excess clamping force can elastically bow a thin or narrow part between the jaws, the same sprung-part effect covered for strap clamps — just produced by vise screw torque instead.
</details>
