## Hand Tapping

Tapping entirely by hand, using a tap wrench rather than a machine spindle or quill, is a core bench skill for jobs where a machine setup isn't practical or a hole simply needs a thread cut at the bench.

**Starting square** is the critical first step: before the tap has cut more than a thread or two, check it against a square held against two adjacent sides of the tap (or the part), confirming the tap is running perpendicular to the surface from more than one viewing angle. A tap that starts even slightly off-square will cut an angled thread for its entire depth, since nothing corrects that error once cutting has begun.

The same principles covered for tapping on a lathe or mill apply directly at the bench: **back off periodically** to break the chip and clear the flutes, especially in a blind hole, rather than forcing continuous advancement until the tap binds.

:::key-concept
A tap has no way to correct its own angle once cutting begins — whatever angle it starts at is the angle it continues at for the rest of the hole. Checking squareness in the first turn or two, before committing to depth, is the only point where an error is still easy to fix.
:::

## Hand Reaming

A reamer turned by hand with a tap wrench or reamer wrench finishes a bore to precision on the bench, the same finishing role covered for machine reaming earlier in this module. The same caution applies here too: **back the reamer out while still rotating forward**, never reversed, to avoid dragging the cutting edges' trailing sides back across the freshly finished surface. Apply steady, even pressure and keep the reamer aligned with the hole by feel throughout the cut.

## Press Fits

A **press fit** (interference fit) joins two parts where the shaft is slightly larger than the bore it's pressed into, relying on the resulting elastic clamping force rather than a fastener or adhesive to hold the assembly together. An **arbor press** — a simple mechanical lever press, sometimes hydraulically assisted for larger work — is the standard shop tool for assembling press fits: bearings, bushings, pins, and dowels.

## Alignment Before Pressing

Getting a press fit started square matters as much as, if not more than, tapping starting square — and for the same underlying reason: once material has started moving under press force, there's no correcting an error, only stopping and assessing the damage.

:::warning
Starting a press fit misaligned, even slightly, can gall or scratch both the bore and the shaft as the misaligned parts bind against each other under press force — damaging both parts before the press force is even fully applied. Confirm the parts are square and aligned before applying meaningful force, not after noticing resistance.
:::

## Supporting the Part Being Pressed Into

The receiving part must be properly supported directly beneath the press force — never press into a part bridging an unsupported span, the same distortion risk covered for clamping in the workholding module, just delivered here as a sudden press load instead of a sustained clamp force. Press smoothly and continuously rather than with sudden, shock-like force, which risks cracking a brittle housing or driving the part in unevenly.

## Bearing Installation

Pressing a bearing in by pushing directly on its outer race, inner race, or — worst of all — through its rolling elements risks damaging the bearing before it's even been put into service. A dedicated **bearing installation sleeve or driver**, matched to whichever race is actually being pressed (outer race when pressing into a housing, inner race when pressing onto a shaft), transmits press force directly through that race's solid material, keeping the load off the rolling elements entirely.

:::pro-tip
If a bearing driver matched to the correct race isn't available, pressing on the wrong race — or worse, through the balls or rollers themselves — can leave brinelling damage (small indentations) that shortens the bearing's service life even though the bearing still turns and looks fine immediately after installation.
:::

**1. Why is checking a hand tap's squareness in the first turn or two so important?**

**2. What caution from machine reaming also applies to hand reaming?**

<details>

Back the reamer out while still rotating forward, never reversed — reversing drags the cutting edges' trailing sides back across the freshly reamed surface and risks scratching it.
</details>

**3. Why can starting a press fit misaligned damage both parts even before full press force is applied?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Once material starts moving under press force, misalignment can't be corrected — the misaligned parts bind and gall or scratch against each other as force builds, before the fit is even fully seated.
</details>

**4. Why should the receiving part be fully supported directly under a press fit, rather than bridging an unsupported span?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The same distortion risk covered for clamping applies here as a sudden press load — an unsupported span can bend or crack under the press force instead of transmitting it cleanly.
</details>

**5. Why is a bearing installation sleeve used instead of pressing directly on the bearing's rolling elements?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Pressing through the rolling elements can leave small indentations (brinelling) that shorten the bearing's service life. A sleeve matched to the correct race transmits press force through solid material, keeping the load off the rolling elements.
</details>
