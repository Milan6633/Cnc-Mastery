## Facing: Cutting the End Square

Facing cuts a workpiece's end flat and perpendicular to its axis, using the cross-slide to feed the tool across the rotating face rather than the carriage feeding along its length. It's usually the first operation on a new piece of stock, establishing a clean, true reference surface (and, often, the Z-zero reference) for everything measured afterward.

Lock the carriage in position before facing by hand — with the carriage stationary, only the cross-slide moves the tool across the face, keeping the cut in a true plane perpendicular to the spindle axis.

## Avoiding the Center Nub

A facing cut can feed either outward from center or inward toward it, but the two directions aren't equivalent in practice. Feeding **inward toward center** requires stopping the tool exactly at true center — fall even slightly short, and a small uncut pip of material (a "nub") is left standing at the exact middle of the face.

:::key-concept
Facing from center outward avoids the center-nub problem entirely: the tool starts at the true center (where accuracy matters most and any error is smallest) and moves outward to the OD, exiting the cut cleanly rather than needing to arrive at an exact stopping point.
:::

:::warning
A nub left at the center of a faced surface isn't just cosmetic — if that face is later used as a locating or reference surface, or if the part is about to be center-drilled at that exact point, the nub sits directly where accuracy matters most.
:::

## Taking the Facing Cut

- Take a light roughing pass first to true up the face, then a lighter finishing pass for the final surface.
- Feed smoothly and consistently — an inconsistent feed rate shows up directly as visible tool marks or an uneven finish on the faced surface.
- Deburr the resulting outer edge; a facing cut typically leaves a sharp edge right at the OD that a standard shop note ("remove all burrs," covered in the blueprint reading module) requires be broken.

## Center Drilling

Once a face is true, a **center drill** cuts a short, stubby pilot hole with a 60° countersink — the same 60° angle a lathe center is ground to (covered in the workholding module's lesson on between-centers turning). This hole either supports the part on a tailstock center for further work, or simply gives a following twist drill an accurate, wobble-free starting point.

- Mount the center drill in a chuck held in the tailstock quill.
- Run the spindle at a relatively high speed for the small diameter involved, and advance the tailstock handwheel slowly and steadily.
- Use cutting fluid — center drills are short and rigid, which makes them strong against bending but brittle against being forced, and they snap easily if fed too aggressively or if chips pack in the small flutes without lubrication.
- Feel for a light, controlled cut rather than pushing hard; a center drill that's cutting correctly advances smoothly with light, steady pressure.

:::pro-tip
Center drill immediately after facing, using the same tailstock-to-spindle-axis alignment, rather than repositioning the part. Any error introduced by remounting the part between facing and center drilling can throw the center hole off the part's true rotational axis.
:::

## Self-Check Questions

**1. Why does facing inward toward center risk leaving an uncut nub, while facing outward from center doesn't?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Facing inward requires stopping the tool exactly at true center — falling short leaves a small pip of uncut material. Facing outward starts at center and exits at the OD, with no precise stopping point required.
</details>

**2. Why is the carriage locked in position, rather than fed along, during a facing cut?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Facing uses only the cross-slide to move the tool across the face in a plane perpendicular to the spindle axis. Locking the carriage keeps that plane true instead of letting the cut drift along the part's length.
</details>

**3. Why are center drills prone to snapping if fed too aggressively?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

They're short and rigid, which resists bending, but that same stubby geometry makes them brittle against being forced — combined with small flutes that pack with chips without adequate cutting fluid, aggressive feed easily snaps them.
</details>

**4. What angle is a center-drilled hole cut to, and why does that angle matter?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

60°, matching the angle a lathe center is ground to — this is what lets the center hole properly support the part on a tailstock center later, with full contact along the cone face rather than at just the tip or edge.
</details>

**5. Why is it best to center drill immediately after facing, using the same setup, rather than repositioning the part first?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Remounting the part between operations risks introducing error that throws the center-drilled hole off the part's true rotational axis, defeating the purpose of a center-drilled reference.
</details>
