## The Opposite Kinematics of a Lathe

Every lathe operation covered earlier in this module rotates the workpiece while the tool stays relatively still, fed into the rotating part. A vertical mill reverses that relationship entirely: the **workpiece stays stationary**, clamped to the table, while a rotating cutter is fed into and across it along up to three axes. Understanding this reversal up front makes the rest of the machine's design make sense — every component exists to hold either the stationary part rigidly, or to move the rotating cutter precisely.

## Column, Knee, Saddle, and Table

- The **column** is the mill's rigid vertical backbone, fixed to the base, carrying the spindle head at its top and providing the structure everything else mounts to or rides against.
- The **knee** rides up and down the column on its own ways, raised and lowered by a knee elevating screw — this sets the overall vertical (Z) working height and, on a manual knee mill, is the primary coarse Z positioning for the workpiece relative to the spindle.
- The **saddle** sits on top of the knee and moves in and out (Y axis, toward and away from the column).
- The **table** sits on top of the saddle and moves left and right (X axis), fitted with T-slots for mounting a vise, clamps, or a fixture plate directly — the same workholding hardware covered in the workholding module.

:::key-concept
On a knee-type mill, X and Y motion come from the table and saddle sliding on the knee, while the knee itself provides coarse Z. This is why the knee, saddle, and table are described together as a stacked system — each one rides on the one beneath it.
:::

## Spindle and Quill

The **spindle**, housed in the head at the top of the column, rotates the cutting tool — it holds the toolholder (via a drawbar through the spindle's taper) and its bearings carry all the radial and axial cutting forces generated at the tool.

The **quill** is a separate, telescoping sleeve that lets the spindle move up and down independently of the knee, over a shorter range, with its own fine feed lever and a quill lock. This gives the mill two distinct ways to control Z position: the knee for coarse, large adjustments, and the quill for fine, controlled plunge motion — critical for drilling, spot facing, and any operation that needs a controlled, feelable downward feed rather than the knee's coarser adjustment.

## Locking Unused Axes

Every axis on a manual mill that isn't actively being fed during a cut should be **locked** before that cut starts. An unlocked table, saddle, or knee can shift slightly under cutting force even when it isn't the axis being fed, introducing play that shows up as a poor finish, an inaccurate dimension, or in the worst case a sudden shift that breaks a tool.

:::warning
Cutting with the knee, saddle, or table unlocked in an axis you're not actively feeding is a common source of chatter and dimensional inconsistency that looks like a tooling or speed/feed problem but is actually a rigidity problem. Lock every axis you aren't using before the cut, not just the ones you remember to check.
:::

## Basic Controls

- **Spindle speed** is set through a belt-position selector or a variable-speed dial, depending on the machine, and should always be changed with the spindle stopped (or, on variable-speed machines designed for it, per the manufacturer's specific procedure).
- **Quill feed** — either a hand lever for manual plunge control, or an engaged power feed for a consistent drilling or plunging rate.
- **Table power feed**, where fitted, drives the X axis at a set rate, freeing the operator from continuously cranking the table handwheel through a long cut.
- A **digital readout (DRO)**, where fitted, tracks table, saddle, and knee position directly, supplementing (or replacing reliance on) the machine's mechanical handwheel dials.

## Self-Check Questions

**1. How does a vertical mill's basic kinematic relationship between tool and workpiece differ from a lathe's?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

On a mill, the workpiece stays stationary, clamped to the table, while a rotating cutter is fed into and across it. On a lathe, the workpiece rotates while the tool is fed into the rotating part.
</details>

**2. What two separate mechanisms provide Z-axis motion on a knee-type mill, and how do they differ in purpose?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The knee provides coarse Z adjustment by riding up and down the column. The quill provides a shorter-range, fine, controlled plunge motion independent of the knee — used for drilling and any operation needing feelable downward feed.
</details>

**3. Why should axes not currently being fed be locked before a cut?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

An unlocked axis can shift slightly under cutting force even when it isn't the one being actively fed, introducing play that causes chatter or dimensional error that can look like a tooling problem but is actually a rigidity problem.
</details>

**4. What rides on what, in order, from the column down to the workpiece on a knee-type mill?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The knee rides on the column; the saddle rides on the knee; the table rides on the saddle; the workpiece is clamped to the table.
</details>

**5. What does a digital readout (DRO) add to a manual mill's existing mechanical dials?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

It tracks table, saddle, and knee position directly and digitally, supplementing or reducing reliance on reading the machine's mechanical handwheel dials.
</details>
