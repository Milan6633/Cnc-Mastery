## What a Grinding Wheel Is Made Of

A grinding wheel is built from two distinct ingredients: **abrasive grain**, the actual cutting particles, and a **bond**, the material holding those grains together in the wheel's shape. Understanding both separately explains almost everything about selecting and maintaining a wheel correctly.

## Matching Abrasive to Material

- **Aluminum oxide** is the standard general-purpose abrasive for steel and most ferrous materials.
- **Silicon carbide** suits cast iron, non-ferrous metals, and other materials where aluminum oxide isn't the best match.
- **Superabrasives** — cubic boron nitride (CBN) and diamond — handle the hardest materials, including hardened steel and carbide, where conventional abrasives wear too quickly to be practical or economical.

Using the wrong abrasive for a material doesn't just grind slower — it can wear the wheel excessively fast, generate excess heat, or fail to cut cleanly at all.

## Grade: A Property of the Bond, Not the Grain

A wheel's **grade** describes how strongly the bond holds the abrasive grains — it's a property of the bond, not a measure of the abrasive's own hardness.

:::key-concept
A "soft" grade wheel releases dulling grains readily, continuously exposing fresh, sharp abrasive as it works — a self-sharpening action well suited to hard materials, where individual grains dull quickly and need to be replaced often. A "hard" grade wheel holds grains longer, suited to soft materials that would otherwise wear a wheel away too fast without giving each grain a chance to do useful work.
:::

This is counterintuitive the first time it's encountered: a "soft" wheel isn't a weaker or lower-quality choice — it's often the *correct* choice for hard, tough materials specifically because it keeps refreshing its cutting edge.

## Grit Size and Structure

- **Grit size** — coarser grit removes material faster with a rougher resulting finish; finer grit removes material more slowly but leaves a smoother finish. Roughing operations favor coarser grit; finishing operations favor finer grit.
- **Structure** — how densely or openly the grains are spaced within the wheel affects chip clearance; a more open structure gives chips more room to escape, useful when grinding a material or operation prone to loading the wheel.

## Dressing: Restoring Sharpness

Over use, a wheel's abrasive grains dull (glazing) or become clogged with workpiece material (loading), both of which reduce cutting efficiency and can generate excess heat against the part. **Dressing** restores a sharp cutting surface by fracturing the bond slightly, exposing fresh abrasive grain, typically using a diamond dressing tool traversed carefully across the wheel's face.

:::warning
A glazed or loaded wheel doesn't just cut slower — it rubs and generates heat rather than cutting cleanly, which can discolor or metallurgically damage a part's surface even at a light downfeed. Watch for a wheel that seems to be "rubbing" rather than cutting, sparking differently than usual, or leaving a burnt-looking surface — all signs dressing is overdue.
:::

## Truing: Restoring Geometry

**Truing** corrects the wheel's actual geometric shape — restoring roundness and flatness to the face — rather than restoring sharpness. A new wheel needs truing when first mounted, to true it to the specific machine's actual spindle axis rather than trusting the wheel's as-manufactured geometry alone; an in-service wheel may need truing again if it's worn unevenly or gone slightly out-of-round. Dressing and truing are often performed together, in the same pass with the same diamond tool, since both operations use essentially the same technique even though they correct different problems.

<details>

Dressing restores sharpness by exposing fresh abrasive grain; truing restores the wheel's actual geometric shape (roundness and face flatness) to the machine's spindle axis. They correct different problems, even though they're often done together with the same tool.

<details>

The wheel seeming to rub instead of cut cleanly, a change in sparking behavior, or a burnt-looking surface on the part — all pointing to a glazed or loaded wheel rather than an issue with machine setup.
</details>
