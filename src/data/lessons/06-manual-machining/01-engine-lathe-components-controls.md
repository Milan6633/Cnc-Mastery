## The Engine Lathe

The engine lathe is the foundation manual turning machine — a rotating spindle holds and drives the workpiece while a cutting tool, held stationary relative to the rotating part, removes material as it's fed into and along the part. Every CNC lathe automates the same basic relationship this machine exposes directly to the operator's hands.

## Headstock

The **headstock** houses the spindle, its bearings, and the gear train (or variable-speed drive) that sets spindle RPM. It holds the workholding device — chuck, collet, or faceplate — and is the single most rigid part of the machine, since every cutting force generated at the tool ultimately reacts back through the headstock's spindle bearings.

Speed selection is typically done through a combination of **back gears** (for a low, high-torque range) and a direct or belt-driven **high range**, set with levers on the headstock before starting the spindle — never while it's running.

## Tailstock

The **tailstock** sits at the opposite end of the bed from the headstock and slides along the ways to support work of different lengths. Its quill (ram) advances via a handwheel to bring a center, drill chuck, or drill directly into contact with the workpiece, and locks in position once set. The tailstock can also be shifted laterally on its base — the basis of the tailstock-offset taper turning method covered later in this module.

## Carriage, Cross-Slide, and Compound Rest

The **carriage** rides along the bed's ways and carries everything that positions the tool:

- The **cross-slide** sits on top of the carriage and moves perpendicular to the spindle axis — toward or away from the workpiece — controlling depth of cut for facing and for setting turning diameter. It has its own graduated handwheel dial, read directly in the depth the tool will actually cut.
- The **compound rest** sits on top of the cross-slide and can be swiveled to any angle, with its own independent handwheel and dial. Besides fine longitudinal adjustment, an angled compound rest is the standard method for cutting a taper by hand feed, covered in a later lesson.

:::key-concept
Cross-slide and compound rest dials read in radius, not diameter, on most lathes — moving the cross-slide in by 0.5 mm reduces the workpiece diameter by 1 mm, since the cut is being removed from all the way around the part. Forgetting this doubling is one of the most common new-machinist mistakes on a manual lathe.
:::

## Bed and Ways

The **bed** is the lathe's structural backbone, and its precision-ground **ways** are what the carriage and tailstock actually ride on. Every dimension the lathe is capable of holding traces back to how true and undamaged those ways are — a chip, a ding, or accumulated wear in the ways introduces error into every operation performed afterward, since the carriage's path is only as straight as the surface guiding it.

:::warning
Never let a part, tool, or chuck key drop onto the ways. A single ding creates a permanent high spot that the carriage will ride over for the rest of the machine's service life, introducing a repeatable error into every job run on that lathe.
:::

## Lead Screw vs. Feed Rod

A manual lathe carries two separate systems for moving the carriage under power, kept deliberately independent:

- The **feed rod** provides general-purpose power feed for turning and facing, geared to advance the carriage at a set rate per spindle revolution.
- The **lead screw** is a separate precision screw, connected through change gears (or a quick-change gearbox) to advance the carriage at an exact, calculated rate matched to a specific thread pitch — used only for threading.

Keeping these separate protects the lead screw's accuracy: everyday turning and facing wear on the feed rod instead, leaving the lead screw's precision reserved for the one job — threading — where it actually matters.

## The Half-Nut Lever

The **half-nut (split-nut) lever** engages a pair of internal nuts around the lead screw, locking the carriage to it so the carriage advances in exact sync with spindle rotation. This connection is only closed for threading — turning and facing use the feed rod instead — and engaging it at the correct moment, tracked with the threading dial, is what lets a threading tool re-enter the same helical groove on each pass.

## Self-Check Questions

**1. Why does moving the cross-slide in by 0.5 mm reduce a workpiece's diameter by a full 1 mm?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The cross-slide dial reads in radius, but the cut removes material all the way around the workpiece. A 0.5 mm reduction in radius removes 0.5 mm from both sides of the diameter, for a total reduction of 1 mm.
</details>

**2. Why are the lead screw and feed rod kept as two separate systems instead of one?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Keeping them separate protects the lead screw's precision for threading — everyday turning and facing wear accumulates on the feed rod instead, leaving the lead screw reserved for the one job where its accuracy actually matters.
</details>

**3. What does engaging the half-nut lever actually do?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

It closes a pair of internal nuts around the lead screw, locking the carriage to it so the carriage advances in exact synchronization with spindle rotation — used specifically for threading.
</details>

**4. Why does damage to the lathe's ways affect every job run on the machine afterward, not just the part being made when the damage happened?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The carriage rides directly on the ways, so a ding or high spot becomes a permanent, repeatable error the carriage travels over on every subsequent job, not just a one-time mistake.
</details>

**5. What does the tailstock's ability to shift laterally on its base enable?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

It's the basis of the tailstock-offset method of taper turning, angling the centerline between headstock and tailstock centers so a normal turning pass produces a taper.
</details>
