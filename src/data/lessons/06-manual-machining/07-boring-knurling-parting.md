## Boring: Truing a Hole to the Part's Own Axis

A drilled hole follows the drill's own tendency to wander slightly, especially in a deep hole or an interrupted cut — it's rarely perfectly true to the part's actual rotational axis. **Boring** enlarges and finishes an existing hole using a boring bar mounted in the toolpost, cutting on the inside of the hole while the part rotates in the chuck. Because the boring bar's path is controlled by the same cross-slide and carriage as any other lathe cut, a bored hole is true to the part's actual axis of rotation — correcting whatever wander the original drilling introduced.

Boring bar rigidity matters more than almost any other single factor in bore quality: minimize the bar's overhang past the toolholder, the same gauge-length principle covered for milling toolholders, since a long, thin boring bar deflects under cutting force far more than a short, well-supported one — showing up as a bore that's slightly larger than intended or a poor finish from chatter.

:::pro-tip
When a bore's finish or size is inconsistent despite a sharp tool and reasonable cutting parameters, check boring bar overhang before anything else. A boring bar extended further than necessary is one of the most common, and most fixable, causes of chatter in internal turning.
:::

## Knurling: Forming, Not Cutting

A **knurling tool** presses a repeating diamond or straight-line pattern into the workpiece surface using hardened rollers under significant radial force — it's a **forming (displacement)** operation, not a cutting one. The rollers push material up and outward into the pattern rather than removing chips, which means knurling generates much higher radial force against the workpiece than a typical cutting operation of similar depth.

- The workpiece and its workholding need to be rigid enough to handle that force without deflecting or being pushed out of the chuck.
- Two rollers straddle the part symmetrically in most knurling tool designs, balancing the radial force so it doesn't push the workpiece off-center the way a single-sided force would.
- Run knurling at a slower speed than typical turning, with cutting fluid or lubricant, both to reduce tool wear from the high contact pressure and to improve the resulting pattern's sharpness and consistency.

:::key-concept
A knurl is displaced material, not removed material — which is why knurling generates forming forces closer to a press operation than a cutting operation, and why workpiece rigidity matters more here than for an equivalent-depth cutting pass.
:::

## Parting (Cut-Off)

A **parting tool** is a thin blade used either to cut a finished part off bar stock entirely, or to cut a narrow groove. Its geometry — necessarily thin, to minimize wasted material and cutting force — is also its greatest weakness: a parting blade has far less rigidity than almost any other lathe tool, and it's the tool most commonly broken by inattentive setup or technique.

- **Tool height is critical.** Set the parting tool exactly at spindle centerline height. A blade set even slightly above or below center rubs on one face of the cut instead of shearing cleanly, generating extra heat and force that commonly binds and snaps the blade.
- **Feed steadily, without stopping mid-cut.** Pausing feed while the tool is engaged lets heat build up locally and increases the chance of binding when feed resumes.
- **Use cutting fluid** to manage the heat generated deep in a narrow groove where chip clearance is already limited.
- **Reduce spindle speed** compared to a typical turning operation for the same material — parting generates more heat for the material removed than most other operations, given how confined the cut is.

:::warning
A parting tool set off-height is one of the most common causes of a snapped blade in the shop. Verify the tool tip is exactly at spindle centerline — checked against the tailstock center's point, or with a height gauge — before the first cut, not after a blade has already broken.
:::

## Self-Check Questions

**1. Why does boring produce a hole more true to the part's own axis than drilling alone typically achieves?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

A drilled hole can wander from the drill's own tendency to deflect, especially when deep. A bored hole's path is controlled by the same cross-slide and carriage as any other lathe cut, so it's true to the part's actual rotational axis.
</details>

**2. Why does minimizing boring bar overhang matter so much for bore quality?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

A longer, thinner boring bar deflects more under cutting force, producing an oversized bore or chatter-induced poor finish — the same gauge-length deflection principle that applies to any toolholder.
</details>

**3. Why is knurling described as a forming operation rather than a cutting operation?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The rollers displace and push material up into the pattern rather than removing chips, generating radial forces closer to a press operation than typical cutting — requiring more rigid workholding as a result.
</details>

**4. Why does a parting tool set even slightly above or below spindle centerline commonly snap?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

An off-height blade rubs on one face of the cut instead of shearing cleanly, generating extra heat and force that leads to binding and breakage — parting blades already have minimal rigidity to begin with.
</details>

**5. Why should a parting cut be fed steadily without stopping partway through?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Pausing while the tool is engaged lets heat build up locally in the confined groove, increasing the chance the tool binds when feed resumes.
</details>
