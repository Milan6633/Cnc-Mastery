## Drilling on the Mill

The quill, covered as a mill component earlier in this module, gives controlled, feelable Z motion independent of the knee — exactly what's needed for drilling. As on the lathe, **spot drilling first** with a stubby, rigid spotting or center drill establishes an accurate, wander-free starting point before the twist drill engages, and **peck drilling** — periodically retracting to clear chips and let coolant reach the cutting edge — protects the drill in deep holes the same way it does anywhere else in the shop.

## Reaming: Finishing a Hole to Precision

A drilled hole, even a carefully spot-drilled one, isn't accurate or smooth enough for many toleranced fits on its own. A **reamer** finishes a pre-drilled hole to a precise final diameter and a much better surface finish than drilling alone achieves, removing only a small, controlled amount of material.

- The pre-drilled hole must be sized to leave the reamer a small, consistent **allowance** — enough material for the reamer to cut cleanly, but not so much that it's effectively doing a drill's job. Follow the reamer manufacturer's recommended pre-drill size for the specific reamer and material rather than guessing.
- Run reamers at a slower speed than drilling and feed steadily — reaming is a finishing operation, and rushing it undermines the precision and finish it's meant to deliver.

:::warning
Back a reamer out of a finished hole while it's still rotating in the cutting direction, not reversed. Reversing a reamer drags its cutting edges' trailing sides backward across the freshly finished bore surface, risking scratches or marks on the very finish the reaming operation was meant to produce.
:::

## Tapping on the Mill

Tapping on a mill typically uses the quill to keep the tap aligned and square to the hole by feel — either hand-turning the tap with the spindle disengaged, or using a dedicated tapping attachment. The same technique covered for lathe tapping applies directly: back off periodically to break the chip and clear the flutes, especially in a blind hole, rather than continuously advancing until the tap binds.

## Counterboring

A **counterbore** enlarges the top of an existing hole to a specific diameter and a flat-bottomed depth — commonly to seat a socket-head cap screw's head flush with or below a surface. Most counterbore tools carry a **pilot**: a smaller-diameter guide at the tool's tip that rides in the previously drilled hole, keeping the counterbore concentric to that hole automatically rather than relying on the operator to hold position by eye.

:::key-concept
A counterbore's pilot does the same job an edge finder or dowel pin does elsewhere — it uses an existing, already-accurate reference (here, the drilled hole itself) to guarantee the new feature's position, rather than trusting a fresh, independent setup to land in exactly the same place.
:::

## Self-Check Questions

**1. Why does spot drilling before running a twist drill help produce an accurate hole?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

A stubby, rigid spot or center drill establishes a true, wander-free starting point, giving the twist drill an accurate location to follow rather than letting it wander on an unmarked surface.
</details>

**2. Why must a reamer's pre-drilled hole be sized to a specific, consistent allowance rather than just "a bit smaller" than final size?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

A reamer is a finishing tool that removes only a small, controlled amount of material. Too little allowance and it can't cut cleanly; too much and it's effectively being asked to do a drill's job, defeating its purpose.
</details>

**3. Why should a reamer be backed out of a hole while still rotating forward, rather than reversed?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Reversing drags the cutting edges' trailing sides backward across the freshly reamed surface, risking scratches on the finish the reaming operation was meant to produce.
</details>

**4. What does the pilot on a counterbore tool do?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

It's a smaller-diameter guide that rides in the previously drilled hole, automatically keeping the counterbore concentric to that hole rather than relying on the operator to hold position by eye.
</details>

**5. What technique from lathe tapping also applies directly to tapping on the mill?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Backing off periodically to break the chip and clear the flutes, especially in a blind hole, rather than continuously advancing until the tap binds or breaks.
</details>
