## Why Grade Selection Matters

The cutting tool material and grade determine how hard and how tough the edge is. Getting this wrong shows up immediately as rapid wear, breakage or built-up edge. If you could design a perfect cutting material from scratch, it would shrug off flank wear and deformation, take a hit without chipping, stay inert against the workpiece chemically, and survive rapid heating and cooling without cracking. Nothing on the market checks every one of those boxes at once, so grade selection is always a negotiation between them.

## The Material Families

| Family | ISO letters | Main constituent |
|---|---|---|
| High speed steel | HSS | Alloyed steel |
| Uncoated cemented carbide | HW | Tungsten carbide + cobalt |
| Coated cemented carbide | HC | HW substrate + coating |
| Cermet | HT (HC coated) | Titanium carbide/nitride |
| Ceramic | CA, CM, CN, CC | Al2O3, mixed, Si3N4 |
| Cubic boron nitride | BN | Boron nitride + binder |
| Polycrystalline diamond | DP (HC coated) | Diamond |

- HSS runs at the lowest speeds and represents a small share of the program, with good edge security but limited wear resistance at higher speeds.
- Cemented carbide (HW/HC) dominates turning, milling and drilling with an excellent combination of abrasive wear resistance and toughness.
- Cermet (HT) is a titanium-based carbide with a cobalt binder, chemically stable and wear resistant, used for finishing and semi-finishing where close tolerance is required. It has limited toughness.
- Ceramics (CA, CM, CN, CC) offer high hot hardness and are used mainly in cast iron, steel, hardened materials and HRSA, but are brittle and need stable conditions.
- Cubic boron nitride (BN) resists high temperatures at high speeds and is used for finishing hardened steel and roughing cast iron at high speeds.
- Polycrystalline diamond (DP) is extremely wear resistant with long tool life, but decomposes at high temperature and dissolves in iron, so it is limited to non-ferrous and non-metallic materials.

## Hardness vs Toughness

The families sit on a spectrum from hardest to toughest:

:::key-concept
Hardness order (high to low): diamond → cubic boron nitride → ceramic → cermet → coated carbide → high speed steel. Toughness runs in the opposite direction.
:::

Hardness gives wear resistance and deformation resistance; toughness gives breakage resistance. As you move toward a harder material you gain speed capability but lose edge security, so harder grades demand stable, well-clamped conditions.

## Which Material for Which Job

| Family | Typical material groups | Typical operations |
|---|---|---|
| HSS | General, low speed | Low-speed turning, drills, taps |
| Cemented carbide | P, M, K, N, S, H | General turning, milling, drilling |
| Cermet | P (steel), finishing | Finishing and semi-finishing |
| Ceramic | K (cast iron), H (hardened), HRSA | High-speed turning, hard turning |
| CBN | H (hardened steel), hard cast iron | Finish hard turning, rough cast iron |
| PCD | N (aluminium, non-ferrous) | High-volume non-ferrous turning |

## ISO Application Codes P01–P50

Within a material group, grades are positioned on the wear-resistance/toughness axis. The ISO P-area codes describe the operations and conditions each grade is designed for.

| Code | Operations and conditions |
|---|---|
| P01 | The finishing end of the range — internal and external finish passes run fast, with a thin chip, tight tolerances and a smooth result; the setup must be free of vibration for this grade to earn its keep |
| P10 | Still a high-speed grade but with more range: turning, copying, threading and milling all fall inside its small-to-medium chip capacity |
| P20 | Steps down to medium speed and takes on facing work with a small chip area; suited to conditions that run from moderate up to genuinely difficult |
| P30 | A mid-to-low-speed grade for turning, milling and facing where the chip area grows to medium-large and the going gets tougher |
| P40 | Built for slow, heavy cuts — turning, facing, milling, parting and grooving — where the chip area is large and conditions punish the edge |
| P50 | The toughness end of the scale, reserved for the harshest cuts: slow speed, large chip area, and a demand for tool toughness above everything else |

:::key-concept
Low ISO numbers (P01) mean high wear resistance for finishing; high numbers (P40, P50) mean high toughness for roughing. The same P/M/K/N/S/H logic applies to every ISO material group.
:::

:::pro-tip
Choose the lowest (most wear-resistant) code that still survives the conditions. Jumping to a tougher grade than needed sacrifices tool life.
:::

**1. List the cutting tool material families from hardest to toughest.**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Diamond, cubic boron nitride, ceramic, cermet, coated carbide, high speed steel — with toughness increasing in the reverse order.
</details>

**2. What do the letters HW and HC mean?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

HW is uncoated cemented carbide (tungsten carbide + cobalt); HC is cemented carbide with a coating.
</details>

**3. Why is polycrystalline diamond not used on steel?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Diamond dissolves easily in iron and decomposes at high temperature, so it is limited to non-ferrous and non-metallic materials.
</details>

**4. What does a P01 grade suit, and how does it differ from P40?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

P01 suits internal and external finishing at high speed with small chip area and good surface finish. P40 suits roughing — low speed, large chip area, very tough conditions — so it is tougher but less wear resistant.
</details>

**5. Why do ceramic grades need stable conditions?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Ceramics are brittle, so they need stable, well-clamped conditions; mixed and whisker-reinforced grades improve their toughness.
</details>
