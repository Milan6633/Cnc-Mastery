## A Height Gauge Is Only as Good as Its Reference

A height gauge measures vertical distance from a flat reference surface — almost always a **surface plate** — up to a feature on the part. Every reading it produces is only as accurate as that reference surface actually is. This is a different relationship than a caliper or micrometer has to the part: those tools carry their own reference (the closed jaws or anvil-spindle zero) built in, while a height gauge's zero lives entirely in the surface plate underneath it.

:::key-concept
A height gauge doesn't measure in isolation — it measures relative to whatever it's sitting on. A precise height gauge on a worn or dirty surface plate produces an imprecise measurement, no matter how good the gauge itself is.
:::

## Surface Plates

A surface plate is a large, flat reference slab — traditionally granite, valued for its dimensional stability, resistance to corrosion, and the fact that it doesn't develop the burrs a metal plate can pick up from an impact. It provides the master flat reference that height gauges, precision levels, and comparator setups all work from.

- **Grades** — surface plates are manufactured to different flatness grades (commonly labeled by application, such as laboratory/reference grade versus a coarser inspection or toolroom grade), with the tightest grades reserved for calibration work and coarser grades adequate for everyday shop inspection.
- **Checking flatness** — a surface plate's flatness is periodically verified (by a calibration service, using an autocollimator or similar precision method) and re-certified, since even a very hard granite surface accumulates wear over years of use, particularly in high-traffic areas of the plate.
- **Localized wear** — because most everyday use concentrates in the center of a plate, that area wears measurably faster than the edges over the plate's service life; some shops deliberately spread work across the whole surface to slow this uneven wear.

:::warning
Dropping a hardened part, gauge, or tool onto a surface plate can chip or dent the surface instantly, creating a permanent high or low spot that throws off every measurement taken near it afterward. Treat a surface plate with the same care as any other precision instrument — it's not a workbench.
:::

## Using a Height Gauge

A height gauge combines a vertical column, graduated in the same fine increments as a caliper (vernier, dial, or digital versions all exist, reading the same way as their caliper counterparts), with a sliding jaw or scriber that moves up the column.

**Measuring a step or feature height:**

1. Set the part on the surface plate, on the same reference surface the height gauge itself sits on.
2. Zero the gauge against the surface plate (or a known reference), then bring the measuring jaw or probe up to the feature.
3. Read the height directly off the scale, the same way as a caliper's main-plus-fine scale.

**Scribing a layout line:**

1. Set the height gauge to the target dimension using its scale.
2. Fit a scriber tip in place of the measuring jaw.
3. Slide the gauge across the part (resting on the same surface plate) to scribe a precise line at that exact height.

:::pro-tip
Clean both the surface plate and the part's reference surface before every height gauge setup, not just when the plate looks visibly dirty. A single trapped chip under the part shifts every height measured from that setup by the chip's thickness — small, but easily larger than the tolerance you're trying to hold.
:::

## Self-Check Questions

**1. Why does a height gauge's accuracy depend on the surface plate underneath it, in a way a caliper's accuracy doesn't depend on an external surface?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

A caliper carries its own zero reference built into the tool (closed jaws). A height gauge's zero is entirely defined by the surface plate it sits on — so a worn or dirty plate degrades every reading regardless of the gauge's own precision.
</details>

**2. Why do surface plates tend to wear faster in the center than at the edges?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Everyday use concentrates in the center of the plate, so that area accumulates wear faster over the plate's service life than the less-used edges.
</details>

**3. Why is dropping a part or tool onto a surface plate a serious problem, not just a cosmetic one?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

A chip or dent creates a permanent high or low spot on the reference surface, throwing off every measurement taken near that spot afterward — it's a precision instrument, not a workbench.
</details>

**4. What are the two main steps in scribing a precise layout line with a height gauge?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Set the gauge to the target dimension using its scale, fit a scriber tip in place of the measuring jaw, then slide the gauge across the part (resting on the same surface plate) to scribe the line.
</details>

**5. Why should you clean the surface plate and part reference surface before every height gauge setup, not just when visibly dirty?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

A single trapped chip under the part shifts every height measured from that setup by the chip's thickness — an error easily larger than the tolerance being held, even when the plate doesn't look obviously dirty.
</details>
