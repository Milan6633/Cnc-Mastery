## The Reference Standard of the Shop

A gauge block (often called a "Jo block," after the format's originator) is a rectangular block of hardened steel or ceramic, lapped to an extremely precise, known length, with two opposing faces flat and parallel to a tolerance far tighter than almost anything else in the shop. Gauge blocks aren't used to measure a part directly — they're used to **check and calibrate other measuring tools**, and to build a precise physical reference dimension for setup work.

## Wringing

Gauge blocks stack together through **wringing**: sliding two blocks' lapped faces together under light pressure causes them to adhere strongly enough to lift and handle as a single unit, with no clamp, adhesive, or mechanical fastener involved. This works because the faces are flat and smooth enough that a thin film of air (or a trace of oil) between them creates a strong molecular attraction once the faces are in near-perfect contact.

To wring two blocks:

1. Clean both mating faces thoroughly — any dust, oil residue, or debris prevents a true wring and adds directly to the stack's error.
2. Slide the faces together at a slight angle, then rotate them flat against each other under light pressure.
3. A properly wrung stack resists a firm pull and shows no visible gap or light between the blocks.

:::key-concept
A correctly wrung gauge block stack introduces essentially no measurable error between the blocks — the stack's total length is, for practical purposes, the sum of the individual block lengths. A poor wring (dirt, misalignment) is the most common source of stack error, not the blocks themselves.
:::

## Building a Stack

Gauge block sets are built with specific block lengths precisely so any dimension within the set's range can be built from a small number of blocks, working from the finest digit outward. Select blocks to cancel one digit of the target dimension at a time, starting with the smallest increment, then work up — this minimizes the number of blocks (and therefore the number of wrung interfaces, each a small opportunity for error) needed for any given stack.

:::pro-tip
Fewer blocks in a stack is always better than more, even if a different combination technically also reaches the target dimension. Every wrung interface is a small potential source of error — a two-block stack is inherently more accurate than a five-block stack built to the same length.
:::

## Accuracy Grades

Gauge blocks are manufactured to different accuracy grades, and matching the grade to the job matters:

- **Reference/calibration grade** blocks hold the tightest tolerance and are used to calibrate other gauge blocks and master equipment — rarely handled on the shop floor directly.
- **Inspection grade** blocks are used to calibrate precision instruments like micrometers and to check critical dimensions.
- **Working (shop) grade** blocks are used for everyday setup work — sizing a fixture, setting a stop, checking a working gauge — where the highest reference-grade accuracy isn't necessary and would only add cost and require more careful handling than the shop floor can guarantee.

## Handling and Care

Gauge blocks are precision instruments, not shop stock, and are handled accordingly:

- Handle by the sides, not the lapped measuring faces — skin oils and contact can affect both flatness and, over time, corrosion resistance.
- Store in the fitted case provided, separated, not loose in a drawer with other tools.
- Never force a wring, and never leave blocks wrung together for extended storage — separate them after use to avoid the wrung faces bonding more permanently than intended or developing surface issues.

## Self-Check Questions

**1. What physical effect allows gauge blocks to stick together when wrung, with no clamp or adhesive?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Extremely flat, smooth lapped faces brought into near-perfect contact create a strong molecular attraction (aided by a thin film of air or oil), strong enough to lift and handle the stack as one unit.
</details>

**2. Why does using fewer blocks in a stack generally give a more accurate result than using more blocks for the same target dimension?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Each wrung interface between blocks is a small potential source of error. Fewer blocks means fewer interfaces, minimizing the opportunity for stack error to accumulate.
</details>

**3. What is the most common source of error in a gauge block stack — the blocks themselves, or the wring?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The wring. A correctly wrung stack introduces essentially no measurable error; dirt, debris, or a poor wring is the far more likely source of stack error than the manufactured blocks.
</details>

**4. Why would a shop use working-grade blocks for everyday setup instead of reference-grade blocks?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Reference-grade accuracy isn't necessary for routine setup tasks like sizing a fixture or setting a stop, and using it would add unnecessary cost while requiring handling care the shop floor can't reliably guarantee.
</details>

**5. Why should gauge blocks be handled by their sides rather than their lapped measuring faces?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Skin oils and contact on the lapped faces can affect flatness and corrosion resistance over time, degrading the very accuracy that makes the blocks useful.
</details>
