## Measuring Deviation, Not Absolute Size

A dial indicator or test indicator doesn't measure a dimension directly the way a caliper or micrometer does — it measures **deviation from a zero reference**, tracking how much a surface moves as the tool sweeps across it or as a part rotates. That makes it the right tool for tramming, centering, checking flatness, and checking runout — jobs where what matters is *change*, not an absolute size.

## Plunger Dial Indicators

A plunger dial indicator has a spring-loaded plunger that moves in a straight line as it contacts the part. Internally, a rack-and-pinion and gear train (the same principle as a dial caliper) convert that linear plunger travel into a rotating needle on the dial face — a common resolution is 0.01 mm per graduation, with a travel range of several millimetres to a few centimetres depending on the model.

Mounted on a magnetic base or an indicator stand, a plunger indicator is the standard choice for checking flatness across a surface or tracking a part's position as a machine axis moves.

## Lever (Test) Indicators

A lever, or test, indicator uses a small sweeping arm instead of a straight-line plunger — the tip contacts the part and the arm's rotation, rather than linear travel, drives the needle. This gives a lever indicator two practical advantages a plunger indicator doesn't have:

- It can be angled to reach into tight spaces a straight plunger can't approach directly — indicating a vise jaw, or the inside of a small bore, for example.
- It's extremely sensitive over a small range, which is exactly what's needed for indicating in a part edge or checking a small runout.

Its tradeoff is a shorter useful travel range than a typical plunger indicator, and its reading changes slightly depending on the sweep angle of the arm — a detail to be aware of on the most precise work, though usually negligible for shop tramming and centering tasks.

## Preloading Before Zeroing

Never zero an indicator with the plunger or lever at the very end of its travel. **Preload** it first — bring the tool into contact with the part until the needle has moved a small, deliberate amount off its rest position, then zero the dial at that point. This ensures the indicator has spring tension engaged and travel available in both directions as the part or tool moves, rather than immediately running out of travel or losing contact the instant something moves the "wrong" way.

:::key-concept
An indicator zeroed at the very start of its travel, with no preload, can only measure deviation in one direction before running out of range — and any small amount of positive movement in the other direction loses contact with the part entirely, giving a false reading.
:::

## Tramming and Centering

Two of the most common indicator jobs in the shop:

- **Tramming a vise** — sweep the indicator along the vise's fixed jaw as a machine axis moves, and adjust the vise until the needle holds steady, confirming the jaw runs parallel (or perpendicular, depending on setup) to machine travel.
- **Centering round stock in a chuck** — mount the indicator so its tip contacts the stock's OD, rotate the spindle by hand, and adjust the stock's position in the chuck until the needle reading stays constant through a full rotation, confirming the stock is running true.

:::pro-tip
Sweep slowly and watch the needle's behavior, not just its final resting number. A needle that jumps, sticks, or moves erratically — rather than sweeping smoothly — is usually telling you about a loose mount or dirty contact point before it's telling you anything about the part.
:::

## Diagnosing a Loose Mount

An indicator is only as trustworthy as what it's attached to. A magnetic base that isn't fully seated, an indicator arm with play in its joints, or a stand that flexes under light pressure will all produce readings that look plausible but don't actually reflect the part — and unlike a wrong caliper reading, a loose-mount error doesn't announce itself. If a reading seems inconsistent between repeated sweeps of the same feature, check the mount's rigidity before doubting the part.

<details>

Preloading ensures the indicator has travel available in both directions once zeroed. Zeroing at the very start of travel means any movement in one direction immediately loses contact or runs out of range.
</details>
