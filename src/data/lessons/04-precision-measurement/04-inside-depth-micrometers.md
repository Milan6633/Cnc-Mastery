## Measuring Internal Dimensions

An outside micrometer can't reach into a bore or a slot — measuring internal dimensions accurately needs a different tool geometry entirely, even though the underlying screw-thread reading principle is the same one covered in the previous lesson.

## Inside Micrometer Types

**Caliper-style inside micrometers** work like an internal version of a caliper — two curved measuring jaws expand against the bore wall, driven by the same kind of thimble mechanism as an outside micrometer. They're quick to use but generally less accurate than a tubular type, and better suited to a fast check than a precision bore measurement.

**Tubular inside micrometers** use a micrometer head fitted with interchangeable **extension rods**, letting one head cover a wide range of bore sizes by swapping rods rather than needing a dedicated tool for every diameter. Assemble the correct rod length for the bore's approximate size, then use the micrometer head's fine adjustment to find the actual diameter.

## The Rocking Technique

Whichever type is used, an inside micrometer measurement is only accurate if the tool is actually spanning the bore's true diameter — and it's very easy to hold it at a slight angle without realizing it, which always measures **larger** than the true diameter (a cocked measurement is always the longest chord across the circle, never the true diameter).

To find the true diameter:

1. Insert the micrometer into the bore and close it to a light contact.
2. **Rock the tool gently side to side and front to back** while feeling for the reading (or the felt resistance) to decrease.
3. The **smallest** reading found while rocking through the bore is the true diameter — any position that reads larger means the tool was cocked at an angle across the bore, not spanning it straight across.

:::key-concept
For an inside bore measurement, the correct reading is always the *minimum* value found while rocking the tool through the hole — never just whatever the tool reads on first light contact.
:::

:::warning
Trusting the first reading an inside micrometer gives, without rocking to find the true minimum, systematically measures the bore as larger than it actually is. On a tight tolerance bore, this can pass a part that's actually undersized.
:::

## Depth Micrometers

A depth micrometer measures how far a hole, slot, or step recedes from a reference surface. Its geometry is effectively an outside micrometer turned inside-out: a **base plate** rests flat on the reference surface, and a measuring rod extends downward through the base into the feature being measured, with interchangeable rods extending the tool's range the same way extension rods do on a tubular inside micrometer.

- Seat the base plate fully and squarely on the reference surface before reading — a base that's rocking or only partially seated introduces the same kind of error a cocked inside micrometer does.
- The reading direction on a depth micrometer's thimble can run opposite to what you'd expect from an outside micrometer, since a longer rod extension (a deeper hole) corresponds to a different thimble rotation direction depending on the specific tool's design — confirm the reading direction against a known depth or the tool's markings rather than assuming it matches an outside mic by habit.

:::pro-tip
Deburr the reference surface before seating a depth micrometer's base. A single burr under the base plate tilts the whole reading, and depth measurements are often checking exactly the kind of shoulder or step where a burr is most likely to be sitting.
:::

## Self-Check Questions

**1. What advantage does a tubular inside micrometer with extension rods have over a caliper-style inside micrometer?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

One micrometer head can cover a wide range of bore sizes by swapping extension rods, and it's generally more accurate — a caliper-style inside micrometer is faster but less precise.
</details>

**2. Why does rocking an inside micrometer through a bore find the true diameter, rather than just reading first contact?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Any angled (cocked) position across a circular bore reads longer than the true diameter, since it measures a chord rather than the actual diameter. The smallest reading found while rocking is the true diameter.
</details>

**3. What happens if you trust an inside micrometer's first contact reading without rocking to find the minimum?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The bore measures as larger than it actually is, since a cocked reading is always longer than the true diameter — on a tight tolerance, this can let an actually-undersized bore pass as acceptable.
</details>

**4. Why must a depth micrometer's base plate be fully and squarely seated before reading?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

A base that's rocking or only partially seated on the reference surface tilts the whole measurement, introducing error the same way a cocked inside micrometer does across a bore.
</details>

**5. Why should you confirm a depth micrometer's reading direction rather than assuming it matches an outside micrometer?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

A depth micrometer's thimble rotation direction relative to depth can differ from an outside micrometer's, depending on the specific tool's design — confirming against a known depth or the tool's own markings avoids reading it backward.
</details>
