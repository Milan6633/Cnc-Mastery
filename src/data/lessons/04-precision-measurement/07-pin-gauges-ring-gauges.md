## Go/No-Go Instead of a Number

A pin gauge or ring gauge doesn't tell you a bore's or shaft's exact dimension the way a micrometer does — it tells you whether that dimension falls within an acceptable range, using feel rather than a scale reading. This trades precision (a number) for speed and simplicity (a pass/fail answer), which is exactly what's needed for fast, repeatable production inspection where reading a dial or digital display for every part would slow the line down.

## Pin Gauges

A pin gauge is a precision-ground cylindrical rod, made to a specific, known diameter and typically supplied as part of a **graduated set** — a full range of pins in small diameter increments (commonly 0.01 mm steps in a metric set), so almost any bore size within the set's range has a matching pin.

To check a bore:

1. Select the pin matching the bore's nominal (target) diameter.
2. Try to insert it into the bore by hand, without forcing.
3. A pin that slides in with a light, consistent drag across the full depth confirms the bore is close to that pin's diameter.
4. Try the next larger pin (should not enter) and the next smaller pin (should enter more freely) to bracket the actual size more precisely, if needed.

:::key-concept
A pin gauge set turns a bore measurement into a simple bracketing exercise: find the largest pin that still enters and the smallest pin that doesn't, and the true bore diameter lies between them.
:::

## Ring Gauges

A ring gauge is the external equivalent of a pin gauge — a precision-bored ring with a known internal diameter, used to check a **shaft's** OD rather than a bore. Ring gauges are often supplied as matched **Go/No-Go pairs**:

- The **Go** ring gauge is sized to the maximum acceptable shaft diameter — it should slide over the shaft.
- The **No-Go** ring gauge is sized to the minimum unacceptable (oversized) diameter — it should **not** slide over a good part.

A shaft that passes the Go gauge and fails (doesn't fit through) the No-Go gauge is within its acceptable size range. This same Go/No-Go pairing logic applies to plug gauges checking bores, covered in the next lesson.

## Reading the Feel

Both pin and ring gauges depend on a trained sense of feel rather than a numeric readout, and that feel has to be learned and applied consistently:

- **Correct fit**: a light, even drag through the full length of engagement — not loose enough to rattle, not tight enough to require force.
- **Too loose**: the gauge falls through freely or rattles with visible play — the feature is oversized (for a pin in a bore) or undersized (for a shaft in a ring).
- **Too tight**: the gauge requires noticeable force to insert or won't seat fully — the feature is undersized (bore) or oversized (shaft).

:::warning
Never force a pin or ring gauge that doesn't fit easily. Forcing it can gall or scratch the gauge's precision surface, damaging a tool that many parts will be checked against afterward, and it also risks damaging the part itself.
:::

## Self-Check Questions

**1. What does a pin gauge tell you that's different from what a micrometer tells you about the same bore?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

A pin gauge gives a go/no-go answer — whether the bore falls within an acceptable size range — rather than an exact numeric dimension, trading precision for speed in repetitive inspection.
</details>

**2. In a Go/No-Go ring gauge pair, what should happen with a shaft that is within its acceptable size range?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

It should pass through (fit onto) the Go gauge, sized to the maximum acceptable diameter, and fail to pass through the No-Go gauge, sized to the minimum unacceptable oversized diameter.
</details>

**3. How can you bracket a bore's actual size more precisely using a pin gauge set?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Find the largest pin that still enters the bore and the smallest pin that doesn't — the true diameter lies between those two pin sizes.
</details>

**4. What does it mean if a pin gauge falls through a bore freely with visible play?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The bore is oversized relative to that pin — a correctly-sized bore gives a light, even drag, not free play.
</details>

**5. Why should you never force a pin or ring gauge that doesn't fit easily?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Forcing it can gall or scratch the gauge's precision surface, damaging a tool used to check many other parts, and risks damaging the part being checked as well.
</details>
