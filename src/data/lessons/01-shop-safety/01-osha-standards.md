## Why Safety Comes First

Before you touch a machine, before you read a blueprint, before you write a single line of G-code — **you learn safety**. This isn't just a formality. Machine shops contain some of the most dangerous equipment in any workplace: spinning metal at thousands of RPM, sharp cutting tools, heavy workpieces, and high-pressure coolant systems.

Every experienced machinist has stories about close calls. The ones who had long careers are the ones who took safety seriously from day one.

> **Key fact:** According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, machinists experience approximately 3.5 injuries per 100 full-time workers annually. Most of these are preventable.

## What Is OSHA?

The **Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA)** is the US federal agency responsible for ensuring safe working conditions. Established in 1970, OSHA sets and enforces standards that employers must follow.

### The General Duty Clause

Even when no specific OSHA standard covers a hazard, the **General Duty Clause** (Section 5(a)(1)) requires that employers provide a workplace "free from recognized hazards that are causing or are likely to cause death or serious physical harm."

This means: if something is obviously dangerous, the employer can't ignore it just because there's no specific rule about it.

## Key OSHA Standards for Machine Shops

Here are the OSHA standards you'll encounter most often in a CNC machine shop:

### 29 CFR 1910.212 — General Requirements for All Machines

This is the big one. It covers:

- **Point of operation guarding** — every machine must have guards that prevent the operator from reaching into dangerous areas
- **Anchoring** — machines must be securely anchored to prevent walking or tipping
- **Guarding by location** — if a machine is positioned so people could walk into moving parts, it must be guarded

### 29 CFR 1910.147 — Lockout/Tagout (LOTO)

This standard covers the control of hazardous energy during maintenance and servicing. We'll cover LOTO in detail in Lesson 4, but the basics are:

- Machines must be completely de-energized before maintenance
- Energy isolation devices must be locked in the "off" position
- Each worker applies their own lock — no exceptions

### 29 CFR 1910.133 — Eye and Face Protection

Machine shops produce chips, sparks, and coolant spray. OSHA requires:

- **Safety glasses** with side shields at all times in the shop
- **Face shields** when grinding or working with high-pressure coolant
- Proper rating: safety glasses must meet ANSI Z87.1 standards

### 29 CFR 1910.95 — Occupational Noise Exposure

CNC machines, especially during heavy cuts, can exceed safe noise levels:

- **85 dB** — action level; hearing conservation program required
- **90 dB** — permissible exposure limit (PEL) for 8 hours
- Hearing protection (earplugs or earmuffs) must be provided

### 29 CFR 1910.1200 — Hazard Communication (HazCom)

Also known as the "Right to Know" standard. This covers:

- **Safety Data Sheets (SDS)** for all chemicals (cutting fluids, coolants, solvents)
- Proper labeling of all chemical containers
- Employee training on chemical hazards

## Your Rights Under OSHA

As a worker, you have the right to:

1. **A safe workplace** — free from recognized hazards
2. **Training** — in a language and vocabulary you understand
3. **Information** — access to injury records, SDS sheets, and OSHA standards
4. **File a complaint** — you can report unsafe conditions to OSHA without fear of retaliation
5. **Refuse dangerous work** — if you believe you face imminent danger (specific conditions apply)

## Common Machine Shop Hazards

| Hazard Category | Examples | OSHA Standard |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanical | Rotating spindles, flying chips, entanglement | 1910.212 |
| Electrical | Exposed wiring, wet floors near electrical | 1910.303 |
| Chemical | Cutting fluid mist, solvent vapors | 1910.1200 |
| Noise | High-speed machining, grinding | 1910.95 |
| Ergonomic | Repetitive motion, heavy lifting | General Duty Clause |
| Slip/Trip/Fall | Coolant on floors, chips, cables | 1910.22 |

## Self-Check Questions

**1. What is the OSHA General Duty Clause?**

The General Duty Clause (Section 5(a)(1)) requires employers to provide a workplace free from recognized hazards that are causing or likely to cause death or serious physical harm — even when no specific OSHA standard addresses the hazard.

**2. At what noise level does OSHA require a hearing conservation program?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

85 dB (decibels) — the action level. At this point, employers must implement a hearing conservation program including monitoring, audiometric testing, and providing hearing protection.
</details>

**3. What does the "Right to Know" standard (HazCom) guarantee?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Workers have the right to know about chemical hazards in their workplace. Employers must maintain Safety Data Sheets (SDS) for all chemicals, properly label containers, and train employees on chemical hazards.
</details>
