## Why Material Classification Matters

Every workpiece you load into a machine behaves differently under the cutting
edge. To keep tool selection, cutting data, and geometry decisions consistent,
the industry groups workpiece materials using the ISO standard.

:::key-concept
The ISO standard divides workpiece materials into six main groups. Each group
has unique properties regarding machinability and setup, and each makes
different demands on the cutting tool.
:::

The six groups are identified by a single letter:

| Code | Material |
|------|----------|
| P | Steel |
| M | Stainless steel |
| K | Cast iron |
| N | Non-ferrous |
| S | Heat Resistant Super Alloys (HRSA) |
| H | Hardened steel |

## The Six Groups and Their Industries

Each group dominates different sectors of industry. Knowing where a group is
used helps you anticipate the tooling and setup demands you will face.

### ISO P — Steel

Because steel touches so many branches of manufacturing at once, the P-area
ends up holding the widest spread of component types of any group. Steel is
the broadest and most common group in metal cutting.

### ISO M — Stainless Steel

Look inside a gas or oil plant, a tube or flange shop, or a pharmaceutical
production line, and stainless steel is what you will mostly find under the
cutting edge.

### ISO K — Cast Iron

Automotive parts, machine-building shops, and iron foundries account for most
of the work in the K-area.

### ISO N — Non-Ferrous

Two industries lead the N-area: aircraft manufacturing and the production of
aluminum wheels for cars.

### ISO S — Heat Resistant Super Alloys

Aerospace components, gas turbines, and power generators supply most of the
hard-to-cut materials that land in the S-area.

### ISO H — Hardened Steel

You will run into H-area hardened steel wherever tight tolerances and wear
resistance matter: automotive supply chains, machine-building shops, and die
and mold shops all rely on it.

## Hardness and Tool Life

Hardness is one of the first properties a machinist checks, because it links
directly to how long a cutting edge will survive.

:::key-concept
Hardness ties directly into the machining data, geometry, and grade you
choose, and it has an inverse relationship with tool life: as hardness climbs,
tool life drops and the cutting edge wears away faster.
:::

This is why the six groups are not enough on their own. Within each group,
subgroups are defined by hardness, specific cutting force (kc1), and
metallurgical and mechanical properties.

## Self-Check

**1. Which ISO code represents stainless steel?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

ISO M. The other codes are P (steel), K (cast iron), N (non-ferrous),
S (heat resistant super alloys), and H (hardened steel).
</details>

**2. Which two industries dominate the N-area?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The aircraft industry and manufacturers of aluminum automotive wheels.
</details>

**3. What happens to tool life as workpiece hardness increases?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

Tool life becomes shorter, and wear on the cutting edge becomes more rapid.
</details>

**4. In which ISO group would you place a gas-turbine component made of a heat resistant super alloy?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

ISO S, which covers heat resistant super alloys and titanium used in
aerospace, gas turbine, and power generator industries.
</details>
