## What Is Stainless Steel?

Stainless steel belongs to ISO group M and is a step up in difficulty from
carbon steel.

- Stainless steels are alloyed with a minimum of 11–12% chromium.
- The carbon content is often low, down to a maximum of 0.01%.
- The main alloying elements are Ni (nickel), Mo (molybdenum), and Ti (titanium).

:::key-concept
Chromium in the alloy reacts with the surrounding air to build a thin Cr2O3
skin on the surface. That passive layer is what blocks corrosion — and what
earned the material its name.
:::

## Stainless Steel Families

ISO M is split into families that machine very differently from one another:

| Family | Notes |
|--------|-------|
| Ferritic / Martensitic | Easier chip control of the group |
| Austenitic | Most common; difficult chip control |
| Super-austenitic | Ni content ≥20% |
| Duplex (austenitic/ferritic) | Mixed structure; difficult chip control |

## Machining Characteristics

Stainless steel is more demanding on both geometry and grade than ISO P steel.

- It is a long-chipping material.
- Chip control is fair in ferritic grades, and difficult in austenitic and
  duplex grades.
- Chips off ISO M come out lamellar and uneven, and getting them there takes
  noticeably more force than a comparable cut in plain steel.

:::warning
Expect a rougher ride when cutting stainless: forces climb, built-up edge
tends to form, and the material work-hardens on top of the heat it
generates. Together, that combination chews through cutting edges faster and
makes chip control harder to hold onto.
:::

:::key-concept
ISO M stainless carries a kc1 of 1800–2850 N/mm².
:::

How well the chip breaks is really a function of the specific alloy and its
heat treatment — some grades break cleanly, others fight you the whole way.
