## Cutting With the Tool's Side, Not Its Tip

Every milling toolpath covered elsewhere in this curriculum cuts primarily with the tool's tip or bottom — the flutes at the end doing the work as the tool moves across or into material. **Swarf milling** does something different: it uses the tool's **side (flank)** — the length of cutting edge running along the tool's shank — engaging a tall wall along its full height simultaneously, orienting the tool so its flank lies directly against the surface being cut.

## The Ruled Surface Requirement

Swarf milling only works accurately on a **ruled surface** — a surface generated by sweeping a straight line along some path, such as an extruded or tapered wall, or a twisted blade-like surface where each cross-section is still connected by straight-line elements even as the shape twists. The tool's own cutting edge is straight (or very close to it), so it can only match a surface that's itself genuinely made of straight-line elements at every position along the path.

:::key-concept
A genuinely **doubly-curved** surface — curved in two directions at once, like a dome or a complex freeform shape — cannot be accurately swarf milled, because no single straight tool-flank orientation matches a compound curve. Swarf milling's accuracy depends entirely on the surface actually being ruled; forcing it onto the wrong surface type produces a wall that doesn't actually match the intended shape.
:::

## The Efficiency Advantage

For the right geometry, swarf milling offers a dramatic efficiency advantage over tip-based alternatives: a tall wall that would otherwise require many stepped-down passes with a flat-bottom or ball-nose tool — each pass cutting only a thin height increment — can instead be machined in a single continuous pass, with the tool's flank engaging the wall's full height at once as it's swept along the ruled path.

:::pro-tip
Before committing to swarf milling for a tall wall feature, confirm the wall is genuinely ruled along its actual programmed path — a wall that looks straight from one viewing angle can still have subtle doubly-curved variation that only becomes apparent once the actual surface data is checked carefully. Verifying this in CAM software, rather than assuming from visual inspection, avoids programming a swarf pass that produces an inaccurate result.
:::

## Self-Check Questions

**1. What part of the tool does swarf milling cut with, as opposed to standard milling?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The tool's side (flank) — the length of cutting edge along the shank — rather than the tip or bottom that most milling toolpaths rely on.
</details>

**2. What is a ruled surface, and why does swarf milling require one?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

A surface generated by sweeping a straight line along a path. Swarf milling's straight tool flank can only accurately match a surface that's itself made of straight-line elements at every position — it can't accurately follow a surface that isn't ruled.
</details>

**3. Why can't swarf milling accurately cut a genuinely doubly-curved surface?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

No single straight tool-flank orientation matches a surface curved in two directions at once — a doubly-curved shape has no straight-line elements for the flank to align with.
</details>

**4. What efficiency advantage does swarf milling offer for a tall, ruled wall?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

The tool's flank can engage the wall's full height in a single continuous pass, replacing what would otherwise require many stepped-down passes with a tip-based tool cutting only a thin height increment at a time.
</details>

**5. Why is it worth verifying a wall's actual surface data in CAM before committing to swarf milling, rather than relying on visual inspection alone?**

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

A wall that looks straight from one viewing angle can still have subtle doubly-curved variation not apparent to the eye — programming a swarf pass on a surface that isn't genuinely ruled produces an inaccurate result.
</details>
