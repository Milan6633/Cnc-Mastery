Between-Centers Turning: Live Centers, Dead Centers, and Dogs

Support long or repeat-mounted shafts on true centers, and drive them with lathe dogs, without introducing runout at the center holes.

Learning objectives

  • Explain why between-centers work preserves a shaft's true axis across multiple mountings
  • Compare live and dead centers and when each is appropriate
  • Explain how a lathe dog transmits rotation that centers alone cannot
  • Identify how a poor center hole introduces runout into a finished part

Why Work Between Centers

Between-centers turning supports a workpiece at both ends using centers seated in center-drilled holes (a standard 60° countersink at each end) rather than gripping it with a chuck. This matters for two distinct reasons:

  • Long, slender shafts need support along their length to resist deflection under cutting force — a chuck alone, gripping only one end, leaves a long shaft to whip and chatter.
  • Repeat, true mounting — a part that needs to be turned, removed, and remounted on the exact same axis multiple times (rough turn, then finish turn; or moved from the lathe to a grinder) stays on that same true axis every time, because the center holes themselves — not a chuck’s variable grip — define the axis.
Key concept

A chuck redefines a part’s rotational axis every time it’s clamped, based on wherever the jaws happen to grip. Center holes define a fixed, physical axis that’s identical every time the same holes are used — that’s what makes centers the right choice whenever a part must be remounted true to a previous operation.

Dead Centers

A dead center is a fixed point — it does not rotate — and the workpiece’s center hole rotates directly against it. Because there’s continuous sliding friction at that interface, a dead center needs grease at the contact point to prevent galling or burning, and RPM has to stay low enough to manage the resulting heat. Dead centers give maximum rigidity and accuracy when properly lubricated and run at appropriate speed, which is why they’re still specified for the most demanding precision work despite the speed limitation.

Live Centers

A live center contains its own bearings, so the center point itself rotates with the workpiece — there’s no sliding friction at the contact point at all. This removes the heat and lubrication concerns of a dead center and allows much higher RPM, making the live center the standard modern choice for the tailstock in general turning work.

Driving the Part: Lathe Dogs

Centers only support a workpiece — they don’t drive its rotation. Something has to transmit the spindle’s rotation into the part, and that’s the job of a lathe dog: a clamp that grips the workpiece OD and engages a slot in a faceplate or drive plate mounted on the spindle.

  • A bent-tail dog has a tail that engages directly into a slot on the drive plate.
  • A straight-tail dog uses a separate drive pin rather than a formed tail.
  • A clamp-type dog grips without marking a finished or delicate surface, at the cost of somewhat less positive drive than a bent-tail dog on a rough surface.

Center Hole Quality

Everything about between-centers accuracy depends on the quality of the center holes themselves:

  • An undersized, damaged, or off-axis center hole introduces runout directly into the part — the shaft can only turn as true as the holes it’s spinning on.
  • The center hole’s angle and depth must match the center’s 60° point correctly. A hole that’s too shallow bears only on its outer edge instead of along the full cone face; one that’s too deep bears only at the very tip. Both concentrate contact wrongly and degrade both accuracy and the life of the center point.
Safety warning

A damaged or poorly-drilled center hole doesn’t just cost a little accuracy — it becomes the new reference axis for everything turned afterward. Inspect and, if needed, re-cut center holes before trusting a between-centers setup for precision work.

Self-Check Questions

1. Why does between-centers mounting keep a part’s axis identical across multiple setups, where a chuck doesn’t?

Show Answer

Center holes define a fixed, physical axis that stays the same every time those same holes are used. A chuck redefines the axis based on wherever the jaws happen to grip that time, which can vary between mountings.

2. Why does a dead center require grease and a lower RPM limit than a live center?

Show Answer

A dead center doesn’t rotate, so the workpiece’s center hole slides directly against it, generating friction and heat that grease manages — and RPM must stay low enough to keep that heat under control.

3. Why can’t centers alone drive a workpiece’s rotation?

Show Answer

Centers only support the part on its axis; they don’t transmit torque. A lathe dog, engaging a slot in the spindle’s drive plate, is needed to actually rotate the part.

4. When would a clamp-type lathe dog be chosen over a bent-tail dog?

Show Answer

When the workpiece surface is finished or delicate and shouldn’t be marked — a clamp-type dog grips without marking, at the cost of somewhat less positive drive than a bent-tail dog on a rough surface.

5. What happens if a center hole is drilled too shallow or too deep for the center’s 60° point?

Show Answer

Too shallow, the center bears only on the hole’s outer edge; too deep, it bears only at the tip. Both concentrate contact incorrectly, degrading accuracy and shortening the center’s life.
