Process Design and Documentation: Control Plans and Work Instructions

Write down exactly how a part is controlled and how an operation is performed, so quality doesn't depend on any one person's memory.

Learning objectives

  • Explain what a control plan specifies for a manufacturing process
  • Explain what a work instruction specifies for a specific operation
  • Explain how these documents address the tribal-knowledge risk covered in the ISO 9001 overview
  • Explain what an auditor is actually checking for when reviewing these documents

Documenting the Process, Not Just the Part

A print defines what a finished part needs to be. It doesn’t, by itself, define how the process that produces it is controlled — which characteristics get checked, how often, with what equipment, and what happens if a measurement comes back out of spec. Control plans and work instructions are the two core documents that fill that gap, together forming the actual, written process a quality system depends on.

Control Plans

A control plan specifies, for each step of a part’s manufacturing process, which characteristics are controlled at that step, how they’re measured, what the sampling frequency is, and what the reaction plan is if a measurement comes back out of tolerance. It’s essentially the master roadmap for how quality is actually monitored and maintained throughout a part’s entire production sequence, not just at final inspection.

Key concept

A control plan doesn’t just say “inspect the part” — it specifies exactly what gets checked, at what frequency, at which specific process step, and precisely what happens next if a check fails. This level of specificity is what makes quality control a defined, repeatable system rather than an informal habit that could vary between operators or shifts.

Work Instructions

A work instruction documents the detailed, step-by-step procedure for actually performing a specific operation — how a particular fixture is set up, in what sequence, with what checks along the way — ensuring the operation is performed the same way regardless of which operator or shift actually runs it.

Addressing the Tribal-Knowledge Risk

Both documents directly address the tribal-knowledge vulnerability covered in the ISO 9001 overview lesson: without them, how a part is actually controlled and how an operation is actually performed exists only in specific individuals’ experience and memory, varying unpredictably between people and disappearing if those people aren’t available. Control plans and work instructions convert that knowledge into a written, shared, auditable resource the whole organization can rely on consistently.

Safety warning

A shop that “knows how to do the job” through experienced people but has no written control plan or work instructions is exactly as vulnerable as covered in the ISO 9001 overview — that knowledge isn’t verifiable, isn’t consistently applied, and provides no defense if a customer or auditor asks to see the actual documented process behind a claimed quality result.

What an Audit Actually Checks

When an ISO 9001 or customer audit reviews control plans and work instructions, it isn’t just checking that the documents exist — it’s checking that the actual shop-floor practice matches what’s documented. A control plan that specifies a check the operators don’t actually perform, or a work instruction describing a setup sequence that doesn’t match how the job is really run, fails an audit just as thoroughly as having no documentation at all — the documentation has to reflect genuine, followed practice, not just paperwork that exists for its own sake.

Pro tip

Treat control plans and work instructions as living documents that need to be kept accurate as the actual process evolves, not as a one-time compliance exercise. A document that drifts out of sync with real practice is worse than no document at all, since it creates a false impression of control that an audit — or a real quality problem — will eventually expose.

Self-Check Questions

1. What does a control plan specify for each step of a manufacturing process?

Show Answer

Which characteristics are controlled at that step, how they’re measured, the sampling frequency, and the reaction plan if a measurement comes back out of tolerance.

2. What is the purpose of a work instruction?

Show Answer

To document the detailed, step-by-step procedure for performing a specific operation, ensuring it’s performed the same way regardless of which operator or shift actually runs it.

3. How do control plans and work instructions address the tribal-knowledge vulnerability covered in the ISO 9001 overview?

Show Answer

They convert knowledge that would otherwise exist only in individuals’ experience into a written, shared, auditable resource the whole organization can rely on, rather than something that varies between people or disappears if key people leave.

4. What does an audit actually verify when reviewing a shop’s control plans and work instructions?

Show Answer

That actual shop-floor practice genuinely matches what’s documented — not just that the documents exist, but that they accurately reflect what’s really being done.

5. Why is a control plan or work instruction that’s drifted out of sync with actual practice described as worse than having no document at all?

Show Answer

It creates a false impression of control — the documentation looks like evidence of a managed process, but doesn’t reflect what’s actually happening, which an audit or a real quality problem will eventually expose.
