How Cemented Carbide Inserts Are Made

The powder, pressing, sintering and coating route that turns tungsten carbide and cobalt into a matched insert grade.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the powder-press-sinter route for cemented carbide inserts
  • Explain the roles of tungsten carbide and cobalt, and how content and grain size set hardness vs toughness
  • Trace the coating development timeline and the purpose of CVD and PVD coatings
  • Explain why substrate, coating and geometry must be matched to the application

From Powder to Cutting Edge

A cemented carbide insert is a powder-metallurgical material: hard tungsten carbide (WC) particles held together by a cobalt (Co) binder, often with titanium, tantalum and niobium carbides added. Manufacture is a carefully controlled process that balances geometry and grade so the finished insert matches its application.

The Powder Route

Strip a cemented carbide grade down to its essentials and you find tungsten carbide (WC) doing the cutting and cobalt (Co) holding it all together, usually with small additions of titanium, tantalum or niobium carbide mixed in. Every grade on the shelf comes down to a recipe question: which ingredients, in what ratio, at what particle size.

  1. Milling: the raw powders are milled together to the required grain size.
  2. Spray drying: the milled powder is spray-dried into small, free-flowing carbide granules that vary in size by grade.
  3. The ready-to-press powder is poured into containers.

Pressing

Turning that powder into an insert blank starts with a tool steel die: a cavity shaped to the insert, closed from above and below by matched punches, with a core pin holding the centre feature where one is needed.

The sequence runs like this. Powder drops into the cavity first. Then the top and bottom punches close in from either side, squeezing the loose granules together under a load somewhere in the 20-to-50-ton range until they hold together as a single fragile blank. A robot arm lifts each pressed blank onto a graphite tray for the trip to the furnace, and operators pull samples off the line at random to check the weight lands where it should.

Key concept

Fresh off the press, the compact is roughly half air by volume — the powder particles are only loosely bonded at this stage. It takes the furnace to drive out that porosity and bring the piece to full density.

Sintering

The pressing trays go straight into a furnace and climb to somewhere around 1400°C. At that temperature the cobalt turns liquid and flows between the tungsten carbide grains, gluing the whole structure into a single dense solid as it cools back down.

Safety warning

The piece that comes out of the furnace is noticeably smaller than the one that went in — about 18% smaller in every linear dimension, which works out to roughly half its pressed volume. Die and punch tooling has to be sized larger than the finished insert from the start to land on the correct dimensions after this shrinkage.

Hardness vs Toughness: Cobalt and Grain Size

Two factors set the balance between hardness and toughness:

  • Cobalt content: typically 4–15% by weight in cemented-carbide grades. More cobalt increases toughness but lowers hardness and wear resistance.
  • WC grain size: larger grains also increase toughness at the cost of hardness.
Key concept

Increasing cobalt content and WC grain size together raises bulk toughness but reduces hardness, so the substrate loses plastic-deformation resistance and practical tool life drops.

The Development Timeline

Cutting tool material has developed in clear steps, each raising productivity:

YearMilestone
1957Indexable inserts
1969First coated insert
1980Thick non-ferrous oxide coating
1989Functional gradients
1999New generation coatings
2006A new insert generation

Large productivity gains came in the 1960s and 70s when the first coatings were developed, followed by advanced substrates, new geometries, edge designs, coating techniques and post-treatment of coated edges.

Coating: CVD vs PVD

Coatings add wear resistance and allow higher cutting data.

PropertyCVDPVD
ProcessChemical reaction of gasesMetal vapour condensing on inserts
TemperatureAbout 900°CAbout 500°C
Thickness2–20 µm (can be thick)2–6 µm (thin)
EdgeThicker, more wear resistant, thermally resistantSharper edges, tougher edge line
Pro tip

Choose CVD for thick, wear- and heat-resistant coatings in stable cuts; choose PVD when you need a sharp edge or a tougher edge line.

CVD builds up layers by repeated gas introduction in a furnace, running for roughly 30 hours. PVD ionizes a metal target (commonly titanium) and condenses the vapour onto the cooler inserts.

Substrate + Coating + Geometry = Application

A grade is the mixture of ingredients in the substrate. Thicker coatings give more wear resistance; harder substrates give more deformation resistance. A functional-gradient substrate combines a tough core with a hard, wear-resistant surface. The coating material, thickness and post-treatment are chosen together with the substrate and geometry to suit one application.

1. What are the two main elements of a cemented carbide insert?

Show Answer

Tungsten carbide (WC) and cobalt (Co), often with titanium, tantalum and niobium carbides added.

2. What happens during sintering?

Show Answer

The pressed inserts are heated to about 1400°C, the cobalt melts and binds the tungsten carbide grains, and the insert shrinks about 18% in every direction (about 50% by volume).

3. How do cobalt content and WC grain size affect the grade?

Show Answer

Increasing cobalt content and grain size raises toughness but lowers hardness and wear resistance, reducing plastic-deformation resistance.

4. When was the first coated insert introduced?

Show Answer

The first coated insert appeared in 1969, following the 1957 introduction of indexable inserts.

5. Why would you choose PVD over CVD coating?

Show Answer

PVD runs at a lower temperature (about 500°C), gives thinner coatings that keep a sharp edge, and provides a tougher edge line.
