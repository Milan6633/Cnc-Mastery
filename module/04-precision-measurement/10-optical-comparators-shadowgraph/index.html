Optical Comparators and Shadowgraph Inspection

Inspect a part's profile against a magnified overlay chart on an optical comparator, without touching the part at all.

Learning objectives

  • Explain how an optical comparator projects a magnified silhouette of a part
  • Use an overlay chart to check a profile against its tolerance
  • Explain why comparator inspection suits small, delicate, or complex-profile parts
  • Recognize the limits of a 2D silhouette check

Measuring Without Touching

Every tool covered so far in this module makes physical contact with the part — a jaw, an anvil, a plunger, a gauge pin. An optical comparator (shadowgraph) inspects a part entirely optically: a light source projects the part’s silhouette, magnified, onto a large viewing screen, and the operator compares that projected profile directly against a printed or drawn overlay chart of the correct shape.

This matters most for parts where physical contact itself is a problem — thin, delicate, or easily deformed features that a caliper or micrometer’s clamping pressure could distort — and for complex profiles (form tools, small stamped parts, thread forms) that are far faster to check as a whole silhouette than by taking a series of individual point measurements.

How It Works

  1. The part is mounted on the comparator’s stage, positioned in the light path between the light source and the projection lens.
  2. Light passes around the part, casting its silhouette through a magnifying lens system onto a screen — common magnifications range from around 10× up to 100×, letting very small features be inspected at a comfortable viewing size.
  3. An overlay chart, drawn at the same magnification as the projected image, is aligned on the screen. The chart shows the part’s correct profile along with its tolerance band as a pair of boundary lines.
  4. The operator checks that the projected silhouette’s edge falls within the tolerance band drawn on the chart, across the full profile.
Key concept

An optical comparator turns a dimensional inspection into a visual one: instead of measuring individual features one at a time, the whole profile is checked against a tolerance band in a single view.

Silhouette Limits

A shadowgraph image shows the part’s outline — its 2D silhouette from one viewing direction — not its full 3D form. This makes it an excellent tool for checking profile shape, edge form, and 2D dimensions visible in that silhouette, but it can’t directly verify a feature that doesn’t show up as an edge in the projected view, such as a surface’s flatness or a feature hidden behind another part of the profile from that angle.

Safety warning

A comparator confirms the part’s outline matches the chart — it does not confirm every feature of the part is correct. A profile that projects correctly can still have an out-of-tolerance surface finish, an incorrect hole depth, or another feature the silhouette doesn’t reveal from that viewing angle.

Backlighting vs. Surface Illumination

Most comparator inspection uses backlighting — light behind the part, producing the silhouette used for profile checks. Some comparators also offer surface (front) illumination, lighting the part from the viewer’s side instead, which reveals surface features like scribed lines, stamped markings, or surface defects rather than the outline — a different inspection task using the same magnifying optics.

Self-Check Questions

1. What does an optical comparator project onto its viewing screen, and how is that different from every other tool in this module?

Show Answer

It projects a magnified silhouette of the part using backlighting, inspecting it entirely optically with no physical contact — every other tool in this module makes physical contact with the part.

2. What is an overlay chart, and how is it used?

Show Answer

A chart drawn at the same magnification as the projected image, showing the part’s correct profile with its tolerance band as boundary lines. The operator checks that the projected silhouette’s edge stays within that band.

3. Why is an optical comparator well suited to inspecting thin or delicate parts?

Show Answer

It inspects entirely optically with no physical contact, avoiding the clamping or contact pressure a caliper or micrometer would apply, which could distort a thin or delicate feature.

4. What can’t a shadowgraph silhouette directly verify about a part?

Show Answer

Features that don’t appear as an edge in the projected 2D view from that angle — such as surface flatness, hole depth, or a feature hidden behind another part of the profile.

5. What does surface (front) illumination on a comparator reveal that backlighting doesn’t?

Show Answer

Surface features like scribed lines, stamped markings, or surface defects, rather than the part’s outline silhouette that backlighting produces.
