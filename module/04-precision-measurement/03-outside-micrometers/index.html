Outside Micrometers: Reading and Use

Read a metric outside micrometer to 0.01 mm and use the ratchet stop for consistent, repeatable measurements.

Learning objectives

  • Identify the anvil, spindle, sleeve, and thimble on an outside micrometer
  • Read a metric outside micrometer by combining the sleeve and thimble scales
  • Explain why the ratchet stop matters more than grip strength for accuracy
  • Check and zero a micrometer before trusting a reading

Anatomy of an Outside Micrometer

An outside micrometer measures external dimensions — diameters, thicknesses, part widths — using a precision screw thread to convert rotation into a very small, repeatable linear movement.

  • Frame — the rigid C-shaped body that holds everything else in alignment.
  • Anvil — the fixed measuring face at one end of the frame.
  • Spindle — the moving measuring face, driven in and out by the internal screw thread as the thimble turns.
  • Sleeve (barrel) — the fixed scale printed on the frame, showing whole and half millimetres.
  • Thimble — the rotating scale attached to the spindle, showing the fine reading.
  • Ratchet stop — a friction clutch at the very end of the thimble that slips at a consistent, low torque, so the operator can’t accidentally over-tighten the spindle onto the part.

Reading a Metric Micrometer

A standard metric outside micrometer’s screw thread advances the spindle exactly 0.5 mm per full revolution of the thimble. The sleeve is marked with main millimetre lines and, between them, offset half-millimetre lines. The thimble’s rotating edge is divided into 50 equal graduations — since one full revolution moves the spindle 0.5 mm, each thimble graduation represents 0.5 mm ÷ 50 = 0.01 mm.

To read a measurement:

  1. Read the last fully visible line on the sleeve’s main scale (whole millimetres).
  2. Add 0.5 mm if a half-millimetre line is visible past that main line.
  3. Read where the thimble’s edge lines up with the sleeve’s horizontal reference line, and multiply that thimble graduation number by 0.01 mm.
  4. Add the three values together.
Key concept

The sleeve gives the coarse reading in 0.5 mm steps; the thimble subdivides each of those steps into fifty 0.01 mm increments. Reading a micrometer is just adding the coarse sleeve value to the fine thimble value.

Some micrometers add a vernier scale along the sleeve, giving one more digit of resolution down to 0.001 mm — read the same way as any vernier scale, by finding the one line that coincides exactly.

Using the Ratchet Stop

The ratchet stop exists to remove the operator’s grip strength from the measurement entirely. Advance the spindle using the ratchet (not the thimble body) until it just contacts the part, and let the ratchet slip — it’s calibrated to apply a small, consistent, repeatable closing force every time, regardless of who’s holding the tool.

Safety warning

Closing a micrometer by gripping the thimble body directly, instead of using the ratchet, lets the operator apply inconsistent — usually excessive — force. This can spring a thin part slightly, or simply give a different reading than the next person measuring the same part with proper ratchet technique.

Checking Zero

Before trusting any reading, confirm the micrometer reads zero at its closed position:

  • A 0–25 mm micrometer zeroes by closing the spindle directly onto the anvil (with the ratchet, not by force).
  • Larger-range micrometers (25–50 mm and up) come with a setting standard — a precision rod of the exact length needed to check zero at that range, since the anvil and spindle can’t close far enough to touch directly.

A micrometer that doesn’t read zero at its reference point will carry that offset into every measurement taken with it until it’s corrected or accounted for.

Pro tip

Take more than one reading around a diameter, not just one. A part that’s slightly out-of-round or tapered will show different readings depending on where and in which direction you measure — a single reading can hide a real geometry problem.

Self-Check Questions

1. What does the ratchet stop on a micrometer actually control?

Show Answer

It’s a friction clutch that slips at a consistent, low torque, applying the same small closing force to the part every time regardless of how hard the operator turns it — removing grip strength as a source of measurement variation.

2. On a standard metric micrometer, how much does one full thimble revolution move the spindle, and what does each of the 50 thimble graduations represent?

Show Answer

One full revolution moves the spindle 0.5 mm. Divided across 50 graduations, each thimble graduation represents 0.01 mm.

3. How do you check zero on a 50-75 mm outside micrometer?

Show Answer

Using a setting standard — a precision rod of the exact reference length for that range — since the anvil and spindle can’t physically close far enough to touch each other directly.

4. Why should you close a micrometer with the ratchet rather than by gripping the thimble body?

Show Answer

The ratchet applies a small, consistent, repeatable force regardless of who’s using the tool. Gripping the thimble directly lets the operator apply inconsistent, often excessive, force that can spring the part or vary the reading.

5. Why is it good practice to take several readings around a diameter instead of just one?

Show Answer

A part that is out-of-round or tapered will read differently depending on where and in which direction it’s measured — a single reading can miss that geometry problem entirely.
