Soft Jaws: Machining Custom Jaws

Why and how to machine custom soft jaws in place so a vise grips a specific part with full, accurate contact.

Learning objectives

  • Explain why soft jaws give more accurate, more repeatable grip than hard vise jaws
  • Describe the in-place machining process used to cut a soft jaw profile
  • Apply the practice of match-marking a soft jaw pair to a specific vise
  • Recognize when soft jaws are worth the setup time versus standard hard jaws

Why Soft Jaws

Hard vise jaws are flat, serrated, and generic — built to grip a wide range of stock shapes reasonably well, but perfectly for none of them. A hard jaw gripping a round part touches it along two thin contact lines; gripping an irregular profile, it may only touch at a couple of high points. Every one of those limited contact points concentrates clamping force, marks finished surfaces, and leaves the part free to shift slightly under cutting load.

Soft jaws are aluminum or mild-steel jaws machined, in the vise itself, to the exact negative profile of the part they’ll hold. Because the profile is cut while the jaws are mounted in the actual vise that will use them, the machined shape matches that specific vise’s true closed geometry — including whatever small variation exists between “nominal” and how that particular vise actually closes.

Key concept

A soft jaw doesn’t just match the part’s shape — it matches this vise’s actual center and clamping geometry, machined in place rather than to a print dimension. That’s what makes it more accurate than a hard jaw, not just more form-fitting.

The In-Place Machining Process

  1. Bolt blank soft jaws (aluminum is common — soft enough to machine quickly, hard enough to hold shape and grip) to the vise’s jaw carriers.
  2. Close the vise on a piece of round or precisely-sized keeper stock, or use a spacer matched to the part’s nominal size, so the jaws close to the correct span.
  3. Machine the jaw profile — bore a round pocket with a boring bar for cylindrical stock, or contour-mill a step, pocket, or irregular profile for shaped parts — while the jaws are clamped in that closed position.
  4. Because the cut happens with the vise closed to the part’s actual working span, the resulting profile is true to how the vise will actually hold that specific part, compensating for any small non-parallelism or wear in the vise itself.
Pro tip

Leave a small machining allowance in the soft jaw stock height. Soft jaws wear and get re-cut over a production run’s life — thicker starting stock means more re-machining cycles before the jaws need replacing.

Common Soft Jaw Features

  • Bored round pockets — grip round stock with full-circumference contact instead of two hard-jaw contact lines.
  • Step jaws — a machined shoulder that a part’s flange or step registers against, giving repeatable Z-height location without a separate stop.
  • Pocket jaws — a full profile pocket for an irregular part shape, useful for repeat production runs of one part.
  • Serrated or knurled grip — added to a soft jaw’s contact face when extra grip is needed on a smooth or slippery surface, at the cost of some surface marking.

Match-Marking and Pairing

A machined soft jaw pair is true only to the specific vise it was cut in — swap one jaw onto a different vise, or swap the left and right jaws with another pair, and the geometry no longer matches. Stamp or engrave a match mark on each jaw and its vise before removing them, and store pairs together. Using an unmatched jaw is functionally the same as using a hard jaw with an unknown, unverified profile.

Safety warning

Never assume two soft jaw sets are interchangeable between vises, even identical vise models from the same manufacturer. Manufacturing tolerance and wear mean each vise closes to a very slightly different geometry — a jaw cut for one vise can leave the part measurably off-center or under-clamped in another.

When Soft Jaws Are Worth It

Soft jaws cost setup time up front — machining the profile takes real cycle time before a single production part is cut. They pay that back when:

  • The job runs enough parts that improved repeatability and reduced part-marking justify the setup.
  • The part shape doesn’t grip well or accurately on standard hard jaws (thin walls, irregular contours, finished surfaces that can’t tolerate serration marks).
  • A feature must be machined concentric or symmetric to a diameter the jaws themselves are holding, where hard-jaw contact-line grip isn’t accurate enough.

For a true one-off part, standard hard jaws with careful setup are usually the better trade.

Self-Check Questions

1. Why does a soft jaw grip a round part more accurately than a hard jaw?

Show Answer

A hard jaw contacts round stock along two thin lines. A soft jaw bored to the part’s diameter contacts it around the full circumference, spreading and centering the clamping force instead of concentrating it at two points.

2. Why are soft jaws machined while mounted in the actual vise that will use them, rather than machined separately and installed?

Show Answer

Machining them in place, with the vise closed to the working span, makes the profile true to that specific vise’s actual closed geometry — compensating for any small variation or wear rather than relying on a nominal print dimension.

3. What does match-marking a soft jaw pair prevent?

Show Answer

It prevents installing a jaw pair (or a mixed pair) on a different vise than the one it was machined in, where the untrue geometry could leave the part off-center or poorly clamped.

4. What is a step jaw used for?

Show Answer

A machined shoulder that a part’s flange or step registers against, giving repeatable Z-height location without needing a separate stop.

5. When does the setup time for soft jaws pay off?

Show Answer

On production runs where improved repeatability and reduced part-marking justify the up-front machining time, or when a part’s shape can’t be gripped accurately or safely on standard hard jaws.
